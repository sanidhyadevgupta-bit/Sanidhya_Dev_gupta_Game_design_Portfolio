BeatRunner

Turn the tunnel. Hit the beat. An endless rhythm runner where you rotate a four-sided tunnel so the one safe side lands under you on every beat, to music the game writes live.

02 · VisionVision

You are a prism racing down a tunnel with four sides. Each wall that rushes at you has one glowing side. You rotate the whole tunnel, a quarter turn left or right or a half-turn flip, so the glowing side is under you at the moment the beat lands.

Design pillars

One input, on the beat

Every action is a turn, and a turn only counts inside the timing window. Input off the beat does nothing at all, so mashing never works. game/src/game.js:29

The music is the level

Walls come from the beat grid of the song, and the song's sections shape the walls: holds on chorus chords, doubles on the build's drum fill, tricks in the drop.

Readable at a glance

Three sides are red and one glows. Walls turn green at the perfect moment. Nothing else on screen competes with that read.

Fail fast, retry faster

Three misses in a row end the run game/src/game.js:616, and Run it back is focused on the results screen.

Your music stays yours

Your own songs are decoded and beat-tracked on your device. Nothing is uploaded and there is no account.

Audience and session

The game is for players who like rhythm games and quick arcade runs, such as Piano Tiles, Geometry Dash, Beat Saber or Thumper, and who want to play in a browser tab or on a phone with nothing to install. A run lasts until three misses in a row. How long that takes for real players hasn't been measured yet. One loop of a genre takes between 45 s (Drum & Bass) and 101 s (R&B) data.json genres[].loopSeconds.

Where the idea came from

The first pitch was a four-plane endless runner with a Piano Tiles twist. You shift the whole plane in time with a song, and each run picks a random track from your Spotify or YouTube playlist. The goal was a playable prototype within a week.

Design thinking

Problem: Spotify and YouTube can't supply audio or beat data to a game, and their developer terms forbid it. The core of the pitch, playing to your own playlist, could not be built the way it was first imagined.

Alternatives considered: streaming APIs (blocked by those terms); a fixed licensed soundtrack (no budget, and it goes stale); a local folder of songs with beat detection on the device; generating music in the browser.

What decided it: the game had to work for everyone the moment it loads, including people who don't have music files. So it does both. Fourteen genres are composed live by the game, and "Your music" beat-tracks local files on the device. The live genres became the main way to play, because they never end and the game knows exactly where every beat and section is.

After playtesting: the playtesting so far was the designer playing each build, with no outside testers yet. The asks it produced shaped the game: walls that flash green on the perfect moment, three speeds (now Easy, Normal and Hard), more genres, more "juice" on hits and misses, and menus that look like the game.

03 · Core loopCore loop and flows

Design thinking

Problem: a turn-on-the-beat game punishes you twice if a miss also leaves the tunnel facing the wrong way. You lose the beat, then you can't read the next wall either.

Decision: after a miss the tunnel snaps to the side you should have reached game/src/game.js:616. The next cue still makes sense, and only the strike counts against you.

Alternative considered: a health bar that drains slowly. It would hide how close you are to the end. Three misses in a row is a rule you can say out loud, and any hit wipes the slate.

Design thinking

Why cosmetic only: coins buy runner shapes and trails, never wider windows or slower walls. Scores on the same genre and difficulty stay comparable, the daily challenge stays fair, and a ghost race is always a race against the same walls.

Design thinking

Problem: in a tunnel that spins, "left" could mean the screen's left or the runner's left.

Decision: left and right always rotate the tunnel the way the arrow points on screen, and flip is its own button. Three inputs cover four sides with no diagonal combos.

Green walls: the designer asked for a visible "now" moment. Turning the red sides green during the Perfect window teaches the timing without a timing bar, and it eases in so you see it coming.

Shipping pass: gamepads weren't supported before this pass. Pad play, menu navigation and pad button prompts were added, and a flip key held at the moment of death can no longer press a button on the results screen.

05 · NarrativeNarrative

There is no story. The game's voice lives in its microcopy: "Turn the tunnel. Hit the beat.", "Sounds right", "Run it back", and trophy names such as "Saved by the bubble" and "Not fooled".

Design thinking

Why none: a run lasts minutes, and the music is the content. Cutscenes or dialogue would sit between the player and the next run. Each genre's look, ambience and instruments set a mood instead.

Writing rule: short and second person, with no jargon in menus. The how-to screen teaches the whole game in three steps and four wall cards.

Design thinking

Problem: fourteen genres can't each get hand-built charts, and the music never ends, so the walls can't be authored in advance.

Decision: the chart generator reads the composer's own beat grid. It knows each beat's section, bar and loudness, so walls can follow the song's structure. Holds land on the long chords that open the chorus, doubles ride the drum fill at the end of the build, and fakes arrive once you know the song.

What the data shows: with the same seed, every genre at Normal gets the same wall pattern in loop 1 (53 walls). The genres differ in tempo, sound and look rather than in pattern. On Easy and Hard the counts start to differ, because quiet beats are skipped and fast genres double the gap to keep walls at least 0.36 s apart (Drum & Bass on Hard has 55 walls in loop 1, Pop has 101). Giving each genre its own pattern rules is the clearest next step for variety.

Easy has no fakes or doubles by design: Easy players are still learning to read the glowing side, so tricks that punish reading wait for Normal.

07 · WallsWalls (the obstacles)

There are no enemies. The walls are the opposition, and the AI is the chart generator that places them.

Design thinking

Readability rule: only the safe side ever glows, except for a fake in the moments before its reveal. A fake is never revealed later than 0.55 s ahead, which is more than four Perfect windows of warning, and a blip sounds as it turns red.

Why fakes come late: on Normal they only appear from loop 2, and only in the chorus and drop. A player first learns "turn to the glow", and is then taught to wait for the reveal once they're fluent.

Why walls sink after you pass them: cleared walls flash and sink into their plane, so they never block the camera's view of the next one.

08 · BossesBoss loops

The one rule to learn: the same song comes back faster and higher, with more tricks. Every fourth loop game/src/music.js:262 plays 8% faster game/src/music.js:263 and two semitones higher game/src/music.js:284. Doubles appear in its drop and fakes are 15% more likely. The tunnel tints red and a banner announces it. Surviving it pays 1,000 × your combo multiplier game/src/game.js:646.

Design thinking

Problem: an endless song has no climax, so long runs risk going flat.

Alternatives considered: a separate boss track per genre (fourteen more pieces of music to write) or a plain speed spike (a difficulty jump, not an event).

Decision: reuse the loop and change the things players hear as "bigger": the key goes up, the tempo pushes, and there are more tricks. Because the music itself changes, the boss is felt, not just seen.

Numbers: loop 4 of a Normal chart in the maps above shows the change. In this seed it has more doubles and fakes than loop 1.

Production method

No textures, sprites or generated images ship with the game. Every visual is built from three.js geometry and materials in code: planes, gate frames, the runner's solid and wireframe, and particles. The store art and these pages are composed from real frames of the game. The 7 runner thumbnails in the Locker are rendered by the game from the same meshes.

Design thinking

Why procedural: a one-week prototype by one designer couldn't afford an art pipeline, and the game is about reading colour and timing. Flat colour, glow and particles read at speed, scale to any screen, and keep the zip under 1 MB.

Consistency rule: the four plane colours are the brand. They appear in the logo gradient, the UI accents, the tier colours and the store art, so every surface looks like the game.

Genre themes change only the background, tint and ambience, never the plane colours, because changing those would break the read.

13 · UIUI and screens

UI rules: one primary action per screen, drawn in the brand gradient. Glass panels sit over the live tunnel, so the game never fully disappears. Every screen fits the viewport with no page scroll, from 1920×950 down to a phone in landscape (844×390), and long lists scroll inside their own panel. Keyboard, pad and touch all reach every button.

Captured from the shipped itch build at 1280×720 after two real bot-played runs (Pop, then Rock). The song in Your music is a generated 120 BPM test WAV.

Design thinking

Problem found in shipping: outside the artifact host, the page lacked a margin reset, so every screen scrolled by 8 px. Short screens such as 1366×650, 820×600 and phones in landscape overflowed on most menus.

Fix: screens became fixed frames with inner scroll regions, and compact and two-column layouts were added for short and landscape viewports. An automated audit now checks 16 screens at 6 sizes on every build.

Adaptive behaviour

Fever brings in the full arrangement (chorus and drop parts) in every section except the intro and break. Boss loops shift tempo and key. A miss muffles the music for a moment. Pause suspends the whole audio clock, so the beat and the walls stay locked together. Your own songs are analysed in a Web Worker: a spectral-flux onset envelope (1024-point FFT, hop 256), tempo by autocorrelation between 68 and 185 BPM with a gentle pull toward 120, then beat tracking that follows small drift.

Design thinking

Why generate music: the beat grid is exact because the game wrote it, which makes the timing fair and lets walls follow the song's sections. It also removes licensing and download size: fourteen genres cost about 30 KB of code.

Honest limit: synthesised genres are sketches of their styles, not productions. Each was tuned by ear on the developer's machine, and they haven't had a wider listening test.

Design thinking

Why no engine: the original assumption was Unity, the designer's usual tool. A browser build was faster to iterate on, playable from a link, and small enough for itch.io's HTML player. The cost is that everything a game engine provides, such as input, UI and audio scheduling, was written by hand.

Known gaps

  • The title is a working title.
  • There has been no outside playtest. Difficulty, the genre mixes and the timing windows were tuned by one person.
  • Charts at Normal follow the same pattern rules in every genre (see Levels).
  • Controls can't be rebound, and there is no colour-blind mode. The read relies on glow and position as well as colour, but this hasn't been tested with players.
  • Your-music beat tracking assumes a steady tempo. Songs with tempo changes or rubato will drift; the beat check can only shift the grid or halve or double the tempo.
  • Tested in Chromium only (headless, with SwiftShader). Safari and Firefox, and real phones, still need a check.
  • Progress is per browser. There is no cloud save.

Next steps

  • An outside playtest with five or more players, recording where runs end.
  • Per-genre pattern rules (for example syncopated walls for Reggaeton, triplets for Jazz).
  • Rebindable keys and a high-contrast mode.
  • Test on Safari, Firefox and real phones; then publish on itch.io.
  • Decide the final name.

Lessons

  • Generating the music made fair timing easy, because the game knows every beat it plays.
  • Shipping prep finds real bugs: the 8 px margin, short-screen overflow, a missing gamepad path, and a held key that could press a button after death.
  • Numbers drift from memory fast. Dumping them from the code kept this document honest.
