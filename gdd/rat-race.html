    RAT-RACE

    A game poem about movement without escape. You grow up on a treadmill in 150 seconds, and the belt never stops.

    GenreCasual / endurance, interactive poem
    PlatformBrowser (WebGL), keyboard + touch
    EngineUnity 6 (URP 2D), C#
    TeamSanidhya Dev Gupta, solo
    Length150 s per life, about three minutes with the ending
    DocumentOctober 2026, built from the game's source
    Section 01

    VISION

    Pitch

    RAT-RACE plays like a small arcade game and reads like a poem. A faceless everyperson walks on an industrial treadmill through six stages of life, from crawling infant to stooped elder, while the belt gets faster, the crates heavier and the sky older. There is no score and no game over. A run lasts 150 seconds, then the belt stops, and so do you.

    Design pillars

    1. Movement without escape. The belt always wins in the end. The only freedom is how you walk.
    2. A life in 150 seconds. Difficulty is not a curve to beat but a biography to feel: age, not level.
    3. Feel over score. No number to chase. Feedback is carried by physics, pixels and sound.
    4. Show, don't explain. Meaning lives in props, crate stamps and a few lines of unexplained text in the sky.
    5. Small and honest. A 320 × 180 screen, 32 colours, one machine, one person.

    Audience and session

    Anyone who can open a browser tab and spare three minutes: portfolio visitors, jury members, players who like short reflective pieces. One life takes 150 s plus about 32 s of ending before it resets by itself (made for kiosks and juries).

    Where it came from

    It began as the Game Design 02 project in Semester 02, presented at Jury 02 on 6 May 2026. The brief was a physics-based game "where skill and feel do the talking". The jury version was a casual endurance game: a conveyor belt as external force, a player as agency, falling obstacles as pressure, and stamina as human limitation. In October 2026 it was rebuilt as pixel art and reshaped from a challenge into an experience.

    Design thinking

    How do you say something about a whole life using only physics?

    Problem
    The brief wanted physics and feel to drive outcomes, not scripted events. I wanted the feel to carry a meaning: that being carried along by something that never stops is what a life can feel like.
    Alternatives
    A text-led narrative (too explanatory), a platformer that climbs out of something (suggests escape is possible), a runner with a score (turns it into a contest).
    Decided by
    The treadmill: it is the most honest image of effort. You move, you sweat, you are busy, and you stay where you started. It also needs almost no explanation.
    After play
    The first version was a challenge to beat. Hands-on play made it clear that beating the belt contradicted the idea, so the fight was softened and the trouble was shaped by age instead (sections 3 and 5).
    Section 02

    CORE LOOP AND FLOWS

    Design thinking

    Why does a run end only by time?

    Problem
    The idea is that you cannot win. A fail state would give the player a way to lose, which quietly implies a way to win.
    Alternatives
    A fatigue death at the end of stamina; a score target; a game-over after too many crates.
    Decided by
    The fixed 150 s timeline: everyone gets the same life, and the ending is a statement, not a result.
    After play
    Stamina runs out and you tire, but you keep walking. Crate hits stagger, they do not hurt (0 stamina lost).

    Game-feel systems

    • Grip model. The belt carries the body exactly, however it speeds up, stops or reverses. The only thing you control is your velocity relative to the belt (U), which moves toward your walk speed at a grip rate of 900 px/s² while walking and 1100 while standing, scaled by control squared.
    • Planted feet. Walk cycles are built so the stance foot moves at exactly the belt's speed; the cadence is your speed relative to the belt divided by the stride, so nobody skates.
    • Forgiving jumps. A 0.14 s grace after leaving the ground and a 0.2 s buffer before landing. Jump height is easy at the start and jump cost is small (2 for the infant, 8 for the elder).
    • Lean. Multiplies walk speed by 1.45 and costs more stamina. It needs a walk key, and leaning while walking with the belt costs a little (2/s).
    • Belt reactions. Landings and crates dip the belt (up to 3 px) with a ripple; the belt warns for 1.4 s, then judders for 0.38 s before it reverses.
    • Screen shake. Whole pixels only: 2 px on a reversal, 3 px for a heavy crate, 1 px for a thud or hit.
    • Not used: hit-stop, aim assist and coyote on walls. Nothing in the game needs them.
    Design thinking

    "The character is not properly walking, it is the physics"

    Problem
    The first movement model eased toward a target velocity, so when the belt sped up or reversed the person lagged behind and slid like on ice. Their feet kept stepping at a made-up pace and slipped against the belt.
    Alternatives
    More friction on the existing model; a rigid carry with no stepping at all; sprite animation sped up to hide the slide.
    Decided by
    Treating the belt as the frame of reference: the body is carried exactly, only U is controlled. Then the walk cycle was rebuilt with two-bone IK so the stance foot travels at the belt's speed, and the cadence derived from U and the stride.
    After play
    Measured in game: zero slip across a belt reversal. Standing still carries you to the guard exactly at belt speed. A lurch on stop and reverse (22 and 18 px/s) shows the belt's force without breaking the grip.
    Section 04

    NARRATIVE AND THE POEM

    Premise

    A person is on a treadmill. We do not know who, why, or where it goes. They grow older with every few seconds, the world outside goes from dawn to dusk, and the objects dropped on the belt change their pictures with the age: baby blocks, school bags, textbooks, laptops, invoices, pill bottles. The story is not told. It is walked.

    Act structure

    The lines in the sky

    16 short lines fade in and out, one at a time, pixel by pixel (1.2 s fade, 4.5 s hold). They are placed between the stage's beats so they never overlap each other.

    Endings

    There is one ending. After the last second the belt winds down at 10 px/s², the machine goes dark, and after the belt stops the lines THE BELT STOPS. and SO DO YOU. arrive, then PRESS ANY KEY. The score falls silent; one low bell marks the end.

    Cast

    Writing and sensitivity

    • Second person, present tense, no names: the player and the person on the belt are the same.
    • Capital letters because the pixel font is capitals only. Lines are short enough to read in one glance.
    • The ending refers to death only by implication ("so do you"). Nothing graphic, nobody falls, the belt just stops.
    • Lines are never explained or repeated, and none of them tells the player what to do.
    Design thinking

    How do you say it without a story?

    Problem
    A life has too many details to depict in three minutes, and details would make it one person's story instead of everyone's.
    Alternatives
    A narrator, a diary, an obituary, dialogue boxes.
    Decided by
    Props and a few sparse lines: a backpack says teenage, a briefcase says middle age, a cane says old age. The lines sit in the sky so they read as weather, not as instruction.
    After play
    Lines are drawn only over open sky, above the skyline, in dark slate on the light skies and paper white on the dusk sky, and arrive by dither so they read as weather rather than a pop-up.
    Section 05

    STAGES: SIX AGES, SIX LEVELS

    There are no separate levels: one belt, six stages. Each card below is generated from the game's stage table. The strip is a map of the stage in time: belt speed (amber), expected crate drops (ticks, height = crate size), poem lines (blue bands), and trip rate (red). Expected counts use the mean spawn interval; a real run varies by the random jitter of 0.85 to 1.2.

    Design thinking

    Why six ages and not four phases?

    Problem
    The jury version used four phases (onset, climb, apex, fatigue): an arc of effort. It worked as a difficulty curve but said nothing about who was walking.
    Alternatives
    Keep four phases and change the sprite once; make the sprite age continuously; six discrete ages.
    Decided by
    Six ages with distinct silhouettes and props read at a glance in pixel art, and a stage can carry its own kind of trouble: nothing in infancy, first stumbles in teenage, a pile-up in early adulthood, a quiet but faster belt in middle age, a chore in old age.
    After play
    Everything that used to be scattered (belt speed, crates, stumbles, wear, sky) now lives in one table so a stage can be re-tuned in one place. The phases survive only as the four skies.
    Section 06

    SYSTEMS: THE PRESSURE

    RAT-RACE has no enemies. The opposition is a machine and a handful of rules: the belt, the crates it carries, and the moments the body trips. All three are described here with their real numbers.

    Design thinking

    Pressure that is fair, then gentle

    Problem
    The belt must feel like an unstoppable force and still never feel unfair or cheap.
    Alternatives
    Instant direction changes (cheap), random hazards with no warning (unreadable), steady speed only (nothing happens).
    Decided by
    Every threat is telegraphed: the lamp, a beep and blinking arrow for 1.4 s; a crate's shadow blinks before it drops; trips are rare and soft. The weight of a machine shows in how it moves: crates lag the belt, bigger ones more.
    After play
    Hands-on play showed crates should nudge rather than hurt, pinned-at-the-guard should not drain stamina, and belt reversals should be rare early (20 to 26 s in teenage, none before it).
    Section 07

    ABILITIES, CRATES, STAMPS

    Shared rules

    • Stamina is 0 to 100. Walking against the belt drains 4/s (9/s leaning); leaning without opposing the belt drains 2/s; being pinned drains 0. Anything else restores 16/s (0.85× while walking). Each stage scales regen and drain (section 5).
    • Tired. At zero you are exhausted until 15: walk speed ×0.45, jumps ×0.8 height, no lean. You never stop moving.
    • Crates are solid once released. You can land on one and be carried by it; you cannot destroy one. A guard at each end of the belt breaks any crate that reaches it.
    • Crate sizes are 16, 24, 32 and 40 px, against bodies from 12 to 33 px tall. In infancy, a crate that arrives on an empty belt always carries the baby stamp.

    Crate sizes

    Stamps by stage

    The shell stays the same so danger reads at a glance. The stamp on its face says what life is throwing at you at that age.

    Design thinking

    A body, not a loadout

    Problem
    There are no weapons or pickups, so the verbs have to be few and each of them has to mean something about a body.
    Alternatives
    A double jump, a dash, power-ups: each gives the player more power than the idea allows.
    Decided by
    Three verbs: walk, lean, jump. Lean is the strong but tiring "I am pushing against this" verb. Stamina exists only to make that effort legible, not to be a failure meter.
    After play
    Penalties were cut roughly in half, jumps made nearly free when young, and walking against the belt made far easier than the first pass: the point is the push, not the punishment.
    Section 08

    THE SIX AGES

    Design thinking

    One person, six bodies

    Problem
    The player should feel the body change without reading a stat.
    Alternatives
    One sprite that tints; a stat sheet; six characters.
    Decided by
    One faceless person redrawn at six ages from a single parametric rig, so proportions, gait and props carry the change: a crawl, a skip, a slouch under a backpack, a stride with a satchel, a hunched power-walk with a briefcase, a shuffle on a cane.
    After play
    Stride lengths are now authored in the rig and exported to the game, because the stance foot must travel exactly the belt's distance per cycle or the feet visibly slip.
    Section 09

    SCORING AND PROGRESSION

    There is no score

    The only number on screen is your age, and it counts up by itself. Within each stage the displayed age is a linear interpolation between the stage's first and last age across the stage's duration; the table shows the values.

    Source: Timeline.cs, Age(t) = round(lerp(AgeFrom, AgeTo, progress in stage)). There are no grades, thresholds, or saved records.

    Design thinking

    Taking away the number

    Problem
    A score invites you to optimise, and optimising a life is exactly the thing the piece is about.
    Alternatives
    A distance walked, time survived, a grade at the end.
    Decided by
    Showing only age and the stage name. Stamina is a gauge built into the machine's cabinet and a thin bar over the head only when you are nearly empty.
    After play
    With nothing to chase, players read the room instead of the HUD, which is where the poem lines and the sky do their work.
    Section 10

    ART DIRECTION

    The rules

    • 320 × 180 pixels, 16 per world unit, always scaled by whole numbers (4× at 1280 × 720).
    • 32 colours, locked in the generator. Anything off-palette is a bug.
    • 48 × 48 cells for every life stage; feet pivot at the bottom of the foot so animations swap cleanly.
    • Selective outline: characters get a dark outline, the environment does not, so the person always pops.
    • Everything moves in whole pixels, including shake. No sub-pixel shimmer.
    • Dither is the transition: stage change, poem fades, sky changes and the vignette all use ordered dither.

    Palette

    Neutrals form the machine and city; four skies share the same structure; tan and red are the crates and their cables; mint, yellow, amber and red are rare and always mean something (safe, hazard, direction lamp); skin, blue, green and hair belong to the person.

    Production method

    Every sprite is generated, not hand-drawn, by an original Python pipeline: a parametric figure rig renders each frame from forward kinematics, shades the back and bottom edges from the palette ramps, outlines the silhouette, and packs all 331 sprites into one 2048 × 512 atlas that Unity slices automatically. The method gives consistent proportions across six ages and makes every change a one-line edit. The cost is that hero frames still want a hand-polish pass.

    Walk cycles

    The machine

    Belt wear, rollers and skies

    Art bible and pipeline

    1. Rig: height, torso, thigh, shin, arm, stride and props per age (rr_figure.py).
    2. Pose: walk uses IK and planted feet; other animations are authored poses.
    3. Render: palette indices into 48 × 48 cells, shade, outline.
    4. Pack: build_atlas.py writes the atlas, the slicing manifest and a generated stride table for the game.
    5. Import: an editor script slices the atlas, sets point filtering, 16 pixels per unit and no compression.
    6. Run: the game builds the whole scene in code from atlas sprites, so there are no scene assets to maintain.
    Design thinking

    Small enough to be honest

    Problem
    The piece is about everyone, so the person cannot be someone.
    Alternatives
    A detailed hand-painted character, a silhouette-only figure, a rat (the title's idiom).
    Decided by
    At 320 × 180 a figure is about thirty pixels tall. A face is not possible, so a posture has to carry everything, which is the point.
    After play
    The first palette and the first prototype's pale blue-grey city survived; only the sky changes colour, so one day equals one life.
    Section 11

    UI AND SCREENS

    UI rules

    • One pixel font (49 glyphs, 5 × 7), integer scales only, with a hard drop shadow where it needs to read against the sky.
    • The HUD is the steel beam across the top: AGE nn on the left, the stage name on the right.
    • Stamina is diegetic: a bar in the machine's cabinet window. A small bar over the head appears only below a quarter.
    • The vignette is dithered ink that closes in with low stamina, capped so it never hides the scene.
    • A grain overlay gives a paper feel at low strength.

    Screens, from the real build

    Design thinking

    A HUD that is part of the machine

    Problem
    Floating game UI says "this is a game with meters". The piece should feel like an object you are inside.
    Alternatives
    A classic stamina bar and score in a corner; an overlay of icons; no feedback at all.
    Decided by
    Putting the information on the machine: the beam shows time, the cabinet shows effort, the lamp shows the belt.
    After play
    The over-head bar was reduced to appear only when nearly empty; the vignette was capped so it never blacks the scene out.
    Section 12

    AUDIO

    A music box that grows old

    All sound is synthesised in code when the game starts: 0 audio files ship with it. The score is a sparse music box on a pentatonic scale (semitones from C5: 0, 2, 4, 7, 9, then the same shape an octave higher). Notes random-walk on the scale; each bell rings for about 1.8 s.

    Stage phrases

    Sound effects and adaptive behaviour

    • Machine: a motor hum whose pitch rises from 0.6× to 1.6× with belt speed, plus a rattle that grows with it. They fade out when the belt stops.
    • Body: a heartbeat below 0.42 stamina (every 0.42 to 0.95 s) and a breath every 1.25 s when exhausted.
    • Foley: footsteps (pitched by body size), jump, land, hit, thud, slam, crate release, break; a belt-reversal groan; a cable winch when a crate is lowered; a warning beep.
    • Ending: a power-down (2.4 s, the longest clip) as the belt winds down, then silence, then one low bell.
    • 16 one-shot and loop clips (), plus ten bell notes and six stage phrases.
    Design thinking

    Sound that ages too

    Problem
    A single looping track would sit on top of the piece instead of moving with the person.
    Alternatives
    Recorded music per stage; no music; a generative score.
    Decided by
    A generative music box that drops an octave and slows from a note every 1.2 s to every 2.3 s, with a short phrase at each stage change. No files means a smaller download and an audio language made from the same few ingredients as the pixels.
    After play
    The score pauses when the tab is hidden (the web wrapper suspends audio), and M mutes everything. Known gap: sounds already playing when you mute finish their tail (up to 2.4 s).
    Section 13

    TECHNOLOGY

    Design thinking

    Everything in code, nothing to import

    Problem
    A solo project has to stay re-tunable and portable: the art, the sound and the timeline all change often.
    Alternatives
    Hand-built scenes and prefabs in the editor; audio and sprite files managed by hand; a different engine for a lighter web build.
    Decided by
    One runtime script builds the whole scene from an atlas, with a generator that writes the art and a table that holds the timings. That is why a stage could be re-shaped in minutes and why art, sound and rules never drift apart.
    After play
    The cost is size: the Unity player makes the web build about 45 MB uncompressed (about 14 MB zipped). A lighter engine would be the first thing to revisit.

    Known gaps

    • No gamepad support.
    • Mute does not cut sounds already playing (tails up to 2.4 s).
    • The Unity console prints two engine messages about internal render-pipeline shaders (a copy pass and an HDR debug view) not being supported on a software GPU. The game renders correctly; they come from the engine's own unused passes.
    • Hero frames (walk cycles, the elder, the infant crawl) are scripted and consistent but want a hand-polish pass.
    • Not tested on a physical phone, iOS Safari or with a real gamepad. Touch was verified only with browser emulation.

    Next steps

    • Hand-polish the hero frames and keep a polished atlas separate from the generator's output.
    • Mute that cuts everything at once; pause audio on focus loss inside the game itself.
    • Gamepad support, and a lighter web build.
    • A second life: the same belt played backwards, as a quiet coda.

    Lessons

    • A table beats scattered constants: the whole arc of a life became tunable in one file.
    • Physics that is physically right (no foot slide) reads as "honest" before any art is added.
    • Remove the fight early. Every system that made the game a challenge fought the idea.
    • Keep numbers in one dump. This document's numbers all trace to data-dump.json, generated from the source.
    Design thinking

    What changed my mind most

    Problem
    I kept treating it as a game to be tuned. Every time I tuned for fun, the meaning thinned.
    Alternatives
    Ship a harder, more satisfying game; ship a walking simulator with no pressure at all.
    Decided by
    Shaping trouble by stage and keeping the belt always present, so there is pressure but nothing to win.
    After play
    Hands-on feedback ("it is not supposed to be this challenging") was the single biggest turn, and it set the arc in section 5.
